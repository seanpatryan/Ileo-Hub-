Understanding Your Ileostomy

What is an Ileostomy?

An ileostomy is a surgical procedure where the ileum (part of the small bowel) is brought through the abdominal wall via a surgically-created opening called a stoma. This allows stool to evacuate from the body via the ileum instead of through the anus.

What to Expect

Stoma Appearance

  • Pink or red color
  • Moist and slightly shiny
  • Typically on right side of abdomen
  • Protrudes 2-3 cm above skin (may be flush)

Normal Output

  • Consistency: Loose or porridge-like
  • Volume: 200-700 mL per day
  • Frequency: Empty pouch 6-8 times daily

High Output Stoma

  • Output exceeding 1,000-2,000 mL/day
  • Typically lasts 1-3 days
  • Affects 14-24% of patients

Nutrition & Diet Management

First 4-6 Weeks Post-Surgery

Important

Follow a low-residue, low-fiber diet during initial healing to prevent blockages while the stoma heals and swelling decreases.

Recommended Proteins & Grains

Proteins:

  • Ground meats and poultry
  • Flaky fish
  • Eggs (small amounts initially)
  • Creamy nut butters
  • Dairy products (if tolerated)
  • Tofu

Grains:

  • White bread, refined cereals
  • White rice, white pasta

Recommended Fruits & Vegetables

Fruits:

  • Canned or pureed fruits
  • Peeled cooked fruits
  • Soft ripe bananas (limit 1 small/day)
  • Melons

Vegetables:

  • Well-cooked without skins or seeds
  • Avoid raw and stringy vegetables

Foods to Avoid (First 6-8 Weeks)

  • Nuts, seeds, popcorn
  • Coconut, dried fruits
  • Raw vegetables with skins
  • Whole grains
  • Tough meats or casings
  • Citrus fruits with membranes
  • Mushrooms, corn

Long-Term Dietary Guidelines

Introduction Strategy (After 4-6 weeks)

  • Add one new food at a time
  • Wait to observe effects before adding another
  • Keep a food diary to track tolerance
  • Chew all foods thoroughly to consistency of applesauce
  • Eat smaller, regular meals rather than large portions

May Cause Gas

  • Beans and legumes
  • Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower
  • Onions and garlic
  • Carbonated beverages, beer

May Cause Odor

  • Eggs, fish
  • Asparagus
  • Onions and garlic
  • Spicy foods

May Thicken Output

  • White rice, bananas
  • Applesauce, pasta, bread
  • Smooth peanut butter
  • Cheese

May Increase Output

  • High-fiber foods
  • Spicy foods
  • Alcohol, caffeine
  • Fried foods, high sugar

Key Nutrients to Monitor

People with ileostomies are at higher risk for specific nutritional deficiencies:

  • Vitamin B12: Absorbed in terminal ileum and colon; annual blood tests recommended
  • Iron: Higher risk of deficiency
  • Magnesium: Often requires supplementation
  • Folate: Monitor levels regularly

Supplementation: Take a daily chewable multivitamin with minerals while following a restricted diet.

Hydration & Electrolytes

Critical Importance

Patients with ileostomies lose excessive quantities of water and sodium in the ileostomy effluent compared to normal fecal losses. This creates a chronic state of dehydration and electrolyte imbalance that requires ongoing management.

Hydration Requirements

Daily Fluid Goals

  • Drink 8-10 glasses (2-2.5 liters) of liquids daily
  • Aim for urine output greater than 1,200 mL per day
  • Monitor for stool output less than 1,200 mL per day

Best Fluid Choices

  • Water
  • Oral rehydration solutions (ORS)
  • Sports drinks diluted with water
  • Milk (if tolerated)
  • Unsweetened juices
  • Decaffeinated coffee or tea

Fluids to Limit

  • Caffeinated beverages
  • Alcohol
  • Hypotonic fluids (plain water, coffee, tea, juice) should be restricted to 500-1,000 mL per day to prevent increased output

Oral Rehydration Solutions

Studies demonstrate that isotonic hydration solutions can prevent dehydration and reduce readmissions.

Options include: Pedialyte, Drip Drop, Ceralyte, or homemade solutions as directed by your healthcare provider.

Signs of Dehydration

Seek medical attention if experiencing:

  • Dry mouth and increased thirst
  • Muscle cramping
  • Decreased urination or dark urine
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness
  • Increased fatigue or headache
  • Confusion

Stoma & Skin Care

Basic Stoma Care Principles

The skin around your stoma (peristomal skin) should look the same as it did before surgery. Protecting this skin is essential because ileostomy output is watery, highly alkaline, and contains proteolytic enzymes that can irritate skin.

Incidence: 25-43% of patients with ileostomies experience peristomal skin complications.

Pouching Systems

Types of Systems

  • One-piece: Skin barrier and pouch combined as single unit
  • Two-piece: Separate skin barrier (stays on skin) and pouch

Changing Frequency

  • Empty pouch when 1/3 to 1/2 full
  • Change baseplate once or twice weekly
  • Avoid wearing pouches longer than 5 days

Cleaning Steps

  1. Use warm water for cleaning (soap optional)
  2. If using soap, choose mild product without lotions or oils
  3. Avoid alcohol-based products (cause excessive drying)
  4. Pat skin completely dry before applying new barrier
  5. Do not use oil-based products on peristomal skin

Normal Characteristics to Monitor

  • Stoma: Pink/red, moist, slightly shiny
  • May bleed slightly when cleaned (normal)
  • Small amount of mucus is normal

Contact Healthcare Provider If:

  • Skin becomes red, wet, or irritated
  • Deep sores or ulcers develop
  • Stoma becomes very dark, purple, or black
  • Continuous bleeding at stoma-skin junction
  • Severe swelling or sudden changes in stoma size
  • No output for 4-6 hours with cramping

Managing High Output

Definition & Incidence

High output stoma is defined as output exceeding 1,000-2,000 mL per day. This condition affects 14-24% of ileostomy patients.

Consequences of High Output

  • Water and sodium depletion
  • Secondary hyperaldosteronism
  • Electrolyte imbalances (including hypomagnesemia)
  • Risk of acute kidney injury
  • Dehydration requiring readmission

Immediate Interventions

  1. Fluid resuscitation: Intravenous fluids and electrolytes if severe
  2. Restrict hypotonic fluids: Limit water, coffee, tea, juice to 500-1,000 mL daily
  3. Increase isotonic fluid intake: Use oral rehydration solutions

Medications

Antimotility Drugs (First-line):

  • Loperamide (Imodium): Start with 2mg three times daily before meals
  • Can increase gradually up to maximum 24mg daily

Antisecretory Drugs: H2 antagonists or proton pump inhibitors

Monitoring

  • Track daily output volumes
  • Monitor urine color (should be pale yellow)
  • Regular blood tests for electrolytes and kidney function

Physical Activity & Exercise

Benefits of Exercise

Regular physical activity provides numerous benefits including improved cardiovascular health, increased muscle strength, better mood, and enhanced quality of life.

Research shows: 70-80% of patients report significant improvements in quality of life post-ileostomy, with many describing their stoma as "a second chance at life."

Timeline for Resuming Exercise

First 6 Weeks

  • Walking: Begin almost immediately; start slowly
  • Avoid: Lifting over 8-10 pounds
  • Focus: Protecting healing tissues

6-12 Weeks

  • Gradually increase activity level
  • Begin low-impact exercises with approval
  • Continue avoiding heavy lifting

After 12 Weeks

  • Most activities can be resumed
  • Return to weight training with precautions
  • Medical clearance recommended

Safe Activities by Category

Low-Impact (Safe to Start Early): Walking, gentle stretching, stationary cycling, light resistance bands, swimming with waterproof system

Moderate (Introduce Gradually): Jogging, yoga, Pilates, recreational cycling

Higher-Risk (Special Precautions): Weight training, contact sports, heavy lifting

Preventing Parastomal Hernia

  • Avoid lifting over 8 pounds in first 6 weeks
  • Use ostomy support belt when engaging abdominal muscles
  • Strengthen core muscles gradually with proper technique
  • Consider supportive garments
07

Recognizing Complications

When to Seek Immediate Help

  • Stoma color changes to dark purple or black
  • No output for 4-6 hours with abdominal pain or cramping
  • Signs of severe dehydration (confusion, rapid heart rate)
  • Significant bleeding from the stoma
  • Signs of infection (fever, spreading redness, pus)

Common Complications to Monitor

  • Blockages: May occur from certain foods; often resolve with rest and fluids
  • Skin irritation: Usually from leakage; address pouching system fit
  • Dehydration: Common in hot weather or with illness; increase fluids
  • Parastomal hernia: Bulging around stoma; may require supportive wear

Emotional Wellbeing

You Are Not Alone

Adjusting to life with an ileostomy is a journey. It's normal to experience a range of emotions including relief, grief, anxiety, and hope—sometimes all in the same day.

Support Resources

  • Ostomy support groups (in-person and online)
  • Certified ostomy nurses (WOCNs)
  • Mental health professionals familiar with chronic illness
  • Peer mentoring programs

Tips for Adjustment

  • Give yourself time to adapt
  • Celebrate small victories
  • Connect with others who understand
  • Focus on what you can do, not limitations
  • Communicate openly with loved ones

Returning to Normal Life

Most people with ileostomies return to their previous activities including work, travel, sports, and intimate relationships. With proper care and support, an ileostomy can be the beginning of a healthier, more active life.
